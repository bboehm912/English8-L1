2. What Are Literary Elements?

MN 8.1.1.1
  • The Building Blocks: Just like bricks build a house, literary elements build a narrative world.
  • Universal Components: Every story—whether ancient myth, modern novel, or movie—uses these same core tools.
  • Why We Analyze: Understanding how authors weave elements together helps us become stronger readers and clearer writers.
💡 The "Big 5" Overview

"Plot, Character, Setting, Point of View, and Theme form the foundation of narrative storytelling."

3. Element 1: Plot & Narrative Architecture

MN 8.1.3.3
  • Definition: The sequence of events that make up a story's cause-and-effect structure.
  • The Engine of Plot: Every plot is driven forward by a central conflict (problem to solve).
  • Action vs. Plot: Action is what happens; plot is why it happens and how characters respond.
📈 Key Concept: Cause & Effect

"Events in a strong plot don't just happen 'and then'—they happen 'therefore' and 'but'!"

4. Mapping the Plot Arc (Freytag's Pyramid)

MN 8.1.3.3
  • 1. Exposition: Background info, setting, and character introductions.
  • 2. Inciting Incident: The spark that sets the main conflict in motion.
  • 3. Rising Action: Complications and stakes build intensity.
  • 4. Climax: The turning point and emotional peak of the narrative.
  • 5. Falling Action & Resolution: Loose ends tie up and a new normal is established.
🏔️ Plot Arc Landmark

"Notice how tension rises steeply toward the climax before settling into resolution."

5. Element 2: Characterization & Character Types

MN 8.1.3.3
  • Protagonist vs. Antagonist: The main figure driving the goal vs. the force opposing them.
  • Direct Characterization: The author explicitly tells us traits (e.g., "She was stubborn").
  • Indirect Characterization (STEAL): Revealed through Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, and Looks.
  • Dynamic vs. Static: Characters who transform internally vs. those who remain unchanged.
🎭 STEAL Acronym Tip

"Great writers show character through actions and dialogue rather than just telling!"

6. Element 3: Setting & Atmosphere

MN 8.1.3.3
  • Three Dimensions of Setting: Time (era, hour), Place (geographic location), and Social Environment (culture, rules).
  • Mood Creation: Setting shapes the emotional atmosphere or feeling experienced by the reader.
  • Setting as Character: In many survival or historical stories, the environment acts as an active antagonist.
🌍 World-Building Note

"Change the setting, and you often change the entire conflict of the story!"

7. Element 4: Point of View (P.O.V.)

MN 8.1.6.6
  • 1st Person ("I, We"): Narrator is inside the story; reader sees thoughts directly, but perspective is limited.
  • 3rd Person Limited ("He, She, They"): Narrator focuses closely on one character's experience.
  • 3rd Person Omniscient: All-knowing narrator who sees into every character's mind and secrets.
  • Unreliable Narrator: A speaker whose perspective may be biased, mistaken, or misleading.
👁️ Lens Analysis

"Always ask: Who is telling this story, and what might they be hiding or omitting?"

8. Element 5: Theme vs. Topic

MN 8.1.2.2
  • Topic (1-2 Words): The subject matter (e.g., "Courage", "Friendship", "Revenge").
  • Theme (Full Sentence): The author's central insight or message about life (e.g., "True courage requires standing up even when afraid").
  • Uncovering Theme: Examine how the protagonist changes and what lesson they learn from the climax.
🎯 Golden Rule of Theme

"A theme is never a single word! It must express a complete universal truth."

9. Internal vs. External Conflict

MN 8.1.3.3
  • Internal Conflict: Character vs. Self (wrestling with fear, moral choices, guilt, or identity).
  • External Conflict:
    • Character vs. Character
    • Character vs. Nature / Environment
    • Character vs. Society / System
  • Layered Conflicts: Great literature combines internal and external struggles simultaneously.
⚡ Conflict Engine

"Without conflict, there is no plot tension, no character growth, and no story!"

10. Active Reading & Annotation Strategies

MN 8.1.1.1
  • Pass 1 - Overview: Read for plot arc flow and immediate impressions.
  • Pass 2 - Observational Note-Taking: Track character choices, setting changes, and key dialogue.
  • Margin Symbols: Use ? for questions, ! for turning points, and ★ for key evidence.
  • O.T.I.S. Standard: Always pair highlighters with marginal commentary—never highlight passively!
📝 Annotation Guideline

"If you highlight a sentence, write at least 3 words in the margin explaining why it caught your attention!"

11. Citing Textual Evidence (The Quote Sandwich)

MN 8.2.1.1
  • Top Bread: Introduce context and signal phrase (e.g., "When describing the setting, the author notes...").
  • Filling: Embed the exact short quote with line/page numbers.
  • Bottom Bread: Explain how the quote supports your analytical claim—never leave quotes floating alone!
🥪 The Quote Sandwich Method

"Claim + Context + Quote + Explanation = Solid evidence-based composition!"
